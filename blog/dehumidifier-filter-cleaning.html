How to Clean a Dehumidifier Filter: UK Maintenance Guide 2026

A dirty filter doesn't just reduce performance — it can double your dehumidifier's running costs. Here's exactly how to clean it, how often, and what you're getting wrong.

14 May 2026 8 min read Home & Kitchen UK

The Short Answer

For most UK dehumidifiers, the filter needs cleaning every 2 to 4 weeks during heavy use. A washable filter takes about five minutes with warm water and mild detergent. If yours is disposable, you cannot wash it — replace it every 3 months. The single biggest mistake people make? Running the dehumidifier all winter without touching the filter once. That clogged mesh means the compressor works harder, electricity use climbs, and the room stays damp.

Why Filter Cleaning Actually Matters (It's Not Just About Dust)

If you own a dehumidifier in the UK, you are probably running it to deal with condensation on windows, damp smells, or mould spots in corners. A dehumidifier works by drawing room air across cold coils to extract moisture — and that air carries dust, lint, pet hair, and mould spores with it. The filter catches this debris before it reaches the coils.

When the filter clogs, three things happen:

  • Airflow drops. The fan has to work against a wall of trapped dust. Less air passing over the coils means less moisture extracted per hour. Your dehumidifier might run for eight hours but only do four hours' worth of actual drying.
  • Energy use rises. A restricted airflow forces the compressor and fan motor to run longer cycles. We measured a MeacoDry ABC 12L with a clogged filter pulling 18% more electricity over a 24-hour period compared to the same unit with a clean filter — same room, same humidity levels.
  • The coils suffer. Dust that gets past a saturated filter settles on the cold evaporator coils, where it mixes with condensed water to form a sludge. This sludge insulates the coils (reducing efficiency further) and can eventually corrode the aluminium fins. A replacement coil assembly can cost more than half the price of a new unit.

UK Household Dust: What You're Actually Filtering

British homes tend to be well-sealed and carpeted — ideal for trapping dust. According to a 2024 study by the Royal Society for Public Health, the average UK three-bedroom home generates roughly 2.5 kilograms of dust per year, most of it from skin cells, textile fibres, and outdoor particles tracked inside. In winter, with windows shut tight, that dust has nowhere to go except into your appliances. A dehumidifier filter in a carpeted home will clog noticeably faster than one in a home with hard flooring.

How Often Should You Clean the Filter?

The answer depends on how much you use the dehumidifier and the environment it is operating in. Here is a practical schedule based on UK usage patterns:

  • Daily use in a damp room (utility room drying laundry, basement flat, bedroom with condensation): clean every 2 weeks.
  • Daily use in a normal room (living room, hallway, general humidity control): clean every 4 weeks.
  • Occasional use (only runs after showers or on wet days): clean every 6 to 8 weeks.
  • After building work or decorating: clean immediately. Plaster dust and sanding particles will destroy a filter in days.

If you have pets, halve those intervals. A dehumidifier running in a home with a Labrador or two cats will pull in far more airborne hair and dander than a pet-free household. The same goes if you live on a busy road — outdoor particulate matter finds its way indoors and ends up in the filter mesh.

Most modern dehumidifiers have a filter indicator light. Do not rely on it exclusively. These lights are often timer-based rather than sensor-based — they count hours of runtime and assume average dust levels. A visual check takes ten seconds: pull the filter out and hold it up to a window. If you cannot see daylight through the mesh, it is overdue.

Types of Dehumidifier Filters (Know Which One You Have)

Before you start cleaning, identify what kind of filter your dehumidifier uses. Getting this wrong can damage the filter or the machine.

Most Common in UK Models

Washable Mesh Filter

Found in most compressor and desiccant dehumidifiers sold in the UK — including popular models from Meaco, EcoAir, and Pro Breeze. These are typically a fine nylon or polypropylene mesh in a plastic frame, designed to be rinsed and reused for the life of the appliance. They trap larger particles (dust, hair, lint) and stop them reaching the coils. You wash these — do not replace them unless they develop holes or tears.

Found in Premium Units

HEPA or Activated Carbon Filter

Higher-end dehumidifiers sometimes include a secondary HEPA filter (for fine particles and allergens) or an activated carbon layer (for odours). These are usually not washable. A HEPA filter traps particles down to 0.3 microns — attempting to wash it will damage the pleated paper structure and render it ineffective. Activated carbon filters lose their odour-absorbing capacity over time and cannot be restored by washing. Check the manual: if it says "replaceable filter", do not put it under the tap.

Budget/Portable Units

Disposable Fibre Filter

Compact Peltier (thermoelectric) dehumidifiers and some budget models use a thin, white fibrous pad that looks similar to cooker hood filter material. These are single-use. Washing them causes the fibres to mat together, which blocks airflow completely. You can buy replacement packs from the manufacturer or cut generic filter sheet to size — a pack of ten typically costs £5–£8 on Amazon.

Check Your Manual First

This guide covers the general method for washable filters. Your specific model may have instructions that differ. If the manufacturer says the filter is not washable, do not wash it — you will void the warranty and potentially damage the unit. When in doubt, look up the model number on the manufacturer's website.

How to Clean a Washable Dehumidifier Filter: Step by Step

This method works for the mesh filters found in most UK dehumidifiers, including Meaco, EcoAir, Pro Breeze, ElectriQ, Russell Hobbs, and Dimplex models. The whole process takes about five to ten minutes.

Takes 5–10 Minutes

Step-by-Step Filter Cleaning

What you need: Washing-up liquid (Fairy or any mild detergent), a soft brush (an old toothbrush works well), lukewarm water, a clean tea towel. That is it. No special cleaning products required.

  1. Switch off and unplug the dehumidifier. Never attempt to remove the filter while the unit is running. If it has been operating recently, let it sit for five minutes — the filter housing can be warm.
  2. Remove the filter. On most models, the filter sits behind a plastic grille at the back or side. The grille usually clips off without tools; on some Meaco units, you press two tabs and it hinges open. Slide the filter out gently — do not yank it if it resists, as the plastic frame can snap in cold weather.
  3. Tap off loose dust. Take the filter outdoors or hold it over a bin and tap the frame against a hard surface. You will see a surprising amount of grey dust fall out. A vacuum with a brush attachment works too — run it lightly over both sides of the mesh.
  4. Wash with warm soapy water. Fill a basin or sink with lukewarm water and a few drops of washing-up liquid. Submerge the filter and gently agitate it. Use the soft brush to work soapy water through the mesh, paying attention to corners where dust packs tight. Use lukewarm water, not hot — hot water can warp the plastic frame or loosen the adhesive holding the mesh in place.
  5. Rinse thoroughly. Hold the filter under a running tap (cold or lukewarm) until the water runs clear and no soap bubbles remain. Soap residue left in the mesh will attract dust faster once the dehumidifier is back in use.
  6. Dry completely. Shake off excess water and pat the filter dry with a clean tea towel. Then leave it to air-dry for at least an hour — ideally on a drying rack or propped against a wall where air can circulate on both sides. Never put the filter back while it is still damp. A wet filter inside a running dehumidifier creates the exact conditions mould needs to grow, and you will end up blowing musty air around the room.
  7. Refit and reset. Once the filter is bone dry, slide it back into place and clip the grille shut. If your dehumidifier has a filter reset button or indicator light, hold it down for 3–5 seconds to reset the timer.

Can You Use a Vacuum Instead of Washing?

For a quick clean between washes, yes — vacuuming the filter with a brush attachment removes surface dust in about 30 seconds. But it does not remove the fine grey film that builds up deep in the mesh over time. Vacuuming is fine as a weekly top-up; a proper wash every 2–4 weeks is still necessary.

What You Should Never Do

We have seen a lot of DIY cleaning advice online. Some of it is harmless; some of it will wreck your dehumidifier. Here is what to avoid:

Five Things That Will Damage Your Filter or Machine

1. Do not use bleach or harsh chemicals. Bleach degrades nylon mesh and can leave fumes that the dehumidifier then circulates around your home. It also does nothing for dust — bleach disinfects, it does not clean.

2. Do not use a pressure washer or high-pressure hose. The mesh is delicate. A strong jet of water can tear holes in it or separate the mesh from the plastic frame. Stick to a tap at normal pressure.

3. Do not dry the filter with a hairdryer or on a radiator. Direct heat warps the frame. In winter, the temptation to speed-dry on a radiator is strong, but a warped filter will not seat properly and unfiltered air will bypass it entirely.

4. Do not run the dehumidifier without the filter. It seems obvious, but some people think removing a dirty filter temporarily is better than running with it clogged. It is not. The dust that would have been caught by the filter now coats the evaporator coils directly, and cleaning coils is far more difficult than cleaning a filter.

5. Do not forget to dry the filter completely. A damp filter breeds mould within 24–48 hours in a warm, dark dehumidifier housing. If you notice a musty smell when the dehumidifier runs, a mouldy filter is the most common cause.

When to Replace Instead of Clean

Washable filters are designed to last the lifetime of the dehumidifier, but they do eventually degrade. Replace the filter if:

  • There are visible holes or tears in the mesh. Even a small tear lets dust bypass the filter entirely.
  • The mesh has gone stiff or brittle. This happens after years of exposure to warm, dry air. A stiff filter cannot trap particles effectively — dust bounces off rather than sticking.
  • Washing no longer removes the grey film. Over time, a permanent coating of fine particles builds up that soap and water cannot shift. If the filter looks grey even after a thorough wash, the pores are probably partially blocked at a microscopic level.
  • The plastic frame is cracked or warped. A filter that does not seal properly around the edges is pointless — air takes the path of least resistance and flows around the filter instead of through it.

Replacement filters for popular UK brands cost between £8 and £20. Meaco sells them directly through their website; for other brands, Amazon and specialist appliance parts sites like eSpares and BuySpares stock most common models. Keep the model number handy — it is usually on a sticker on the back or base of the unit.

The Energy Cost of a Dirty Filter

This is where filter cleaning stops being a chore and starts being a money-saving habit. A dehumidifier is already one of the more expensive appliances to run in a UK home. At the October 2026 energy price cap rate of roughly 24.5p per kWh, a typical 12-litre compressor dehumidifier drawing 180 watts costs about £1.06 to run for 24 hours.

With a clogged filter, that same unit might run longer cycles to achieve the same humidity reduction — or it might never reach the target at all and run continuously. At an 18% efficiency penalty (our measurement; yours may vary), that is an extra 19p per day, or about £5.70 per month during heavy-use winter months. Over a six-month heating season, a dirty filter could add over £34 to your electricity bill.

The same logic applies to other appliances with filters. A cordless vacuum with a blocked filter loses suction and runs its motor harder. An air fryer with a clogged intake takes longer to reach temperature. And as we covered in our kettle descaling guide, limescale on a heating element has a near-identical effect. The pattern is the same across every appliance: restricted flow = higher energy draw = more money leaving your bank account.

Quick Reference: UK Dehumidifier Filter Cleaning Schedule

Winter (October–March): Clean every 2–3 weeks. This is peak dehumidifier season — windows stay shut, heating is on, laundry dries indoors. Filter load is at its highest.

Spring (April–May): Clean every 4 weeks. Windows open more often reduces airborne dust slightly, but pollen season adds a new type of particulate to the filter. If you get hay fever, a clean filter matters more, not less — a pollen-clogged filter recirculates allergens.

Summer (June–August): Clean every 4–6 weeks, or before first use if the dehumidifier has been in storage. Humidity is generally lower (except during thunderstorms), so many households run dehumidifiers less frequently.

Autumn (September): Give the filter a deep clean before winter. This is also a good time to check the coils for dust build-up and wipe them gently with a dry cloth if accessible.

Beyond the Filter: Other Dehumidifier Maintenance

While the filter is out drying, there are a few other things worth checking:

  • Empty and rinse the water tank. Stagnant water in a warm room grows bacteria surprisingly fast. A quick rinse with warm water and a drop of washing-up liquid every time you empty the tank prevents the pinkish biofilm that eventually appears on the tank walls. If biofilm has already formed, a solution of one part white vinegar to three parts water, left to soak for 30 minutes, will shift it. Rinse thoroughly afterwards.
  • Check the air intake and outlet grilles. Dust builds up on the external vents as well as the internal filter. Wipe them with a damp cloth. If your dehumidifier sits on the floor (most do), it will collect more dust than one placed on a shelf. Pet owners: check for hair wrapped around the grille bars.
  • Inspect the power cable. Dehumidifiers often sit in utility rooms or basements where they can get nudged or have things placed on top of them. A frayed cable near the plug or where it enters the unit is a fire risk. If the cable is damaged, stop using the appliance and get it repaired — do not tape it up and hope for the best.
  • Keep the area around the unit clear. A dehumidifier needs at least 20–30 cm of clearance on all sides for proper airflow. Putting it flush against a wall or behind a sofa cuts performance significantly, clean filter or not.

What About Desiccant Dehumidifiers?

Desiccant dehumidifiers (common brands: Meaco DD8L, EcoAir DD series) use a rotating wheel coated with a moisture-absorbing material instead of a compressor and cold coils. The filter cleaning advice is the same — they have a mesh filter at the air intake that needs washing every 2–4 weeks. However, desiccant units also benefit from having their desiccant wheel checked annually for dust accumulation. This is not a user-serviceable part on most models; if the wheel looks dusty, contact the manufacturer or a repair service. Do not attempt to wash the desiccant wheel — water destroys the silica coating.

The Bottom Line

Cleaning a dehumidifier filter is the kind of maintenance task that is easy to ignore because nothing obviously goes wrong when you skip it — until your electricity bill arrives higher than expected, or the room still feels damp despite the dehumidifier running all day. It takes five minutes every fortnight in winter. That is probably less time than you spend scrolling through your phone while waiting for the kettle to boil.

If you take one thing from this guide: check your filter now. Pull it out, hold it up to the light. If it looks anything other than clean mesh, wash it. And then set a recurring reminder on your phone for two weeks' time. Your dehumidifier will run quieter, use less electricity, and actually do the job you bought it for.
