How to Choose the Right Microwave

1560|

Choosing a microwave might seem simple, but there are a few key decisions that make the difference between a daily frustration and a kitchen workhorse. Here's what to consider before buying.

1561| 1562|
1563|

Solo vs Grill vs Combination: What Do You Need?

1564|

Microwaves come in three main types, and understanding the differences will save you from paying for features you won't use — or regretting not having them.

1565|
    1566|
  • Solo microwave: The standard. Heats and defrosts using microwave energy only. Perfect for reheating, defrosting, and basic cooking. All five models in our roundup have solo microwave functionality. Best for: most households.
    • 1567|
  • Grill microwave: Adds a heating element (usually quartz or halogen) that browns and crisps the top of food. The Panasonic NN-E27JWMBPQ and Samsung MS23K3515AK both have good grills. Best for: anyone who wants crispy toppings on pasta bakes, jacket potatoes, or cheese on toast.
    • 1568|
  • Combination microwave: Adds convection oven functionality, essentially giving you a second oven. You can roast, bake, and even cook pastry. These are larger and more expensive (typically £180+). Best for: kitchens without a built-in oven, or keen cooks who want an extra oven.
    • 1569|
1570|
1571| 1572|
1573|

Capacity: How Big Should You Go?

1574|

Microwave capacity is measured in litres, and the right size depends on how many people you're feeding and what you typically cook.

1575|
    1576|
  • 1-2 people: 17-20 litres is plenty. The Russell Hobbs RHM2076B and Sharp R272KM are ideal for singles and couples.
    • 1577|
  • 3-4 people (families): 20-25 litres gives you room for larger plates and dishes. The Samsung MS23K3515AK at 23L is our pick for families.
    • 1578|
  • 5+ people or large households: 25+ litres. The Sage the Quick Touch at 25L flatbed gives you the maximum usable space.
    • 1579|
1580|

A useful trick: measure the largest dinner plate or casserole dish you use regularly and check it fits inside the microwave's dimensions before buying. Turntable microwaves lose usable space to the rotating mechanism, so a 20L flatbed can actually be more practical than a 23L turntable model for large rectangular dishes.

1581|
1582| 1583|
1584|

Inverter vs Standard Microwave Technology

1585|

This is the single biggest difference in cooking quality between budget and mid-range microwaves.

1586|
    1587|
  • Standard microwaves pulse power on and off at lower settings. Set it to 50% power and it'll blast at 100% for a few seconds, then go quiet, then blast again. This can lead to uneven defrosting (frozen centre, cooked edges) and inconsistent results with delicate foods.
    • 1588|
  • Inverter microwaves (Panasonic and Sage models) deliver a constant, steady stream of power at any level. At 30% power, you get a consistent 30% stream rather than 100% pulsing. This gives dramatically better results for defrosting fish, melting chocolate, softening butter, and reheating sauce-based dishes without splitting them.
    • 1589|
1590|

The Panasonic NN-E27JWMBPQ is the cheapest inverter microwave available in the UK and our top recommendation if you care about cooking quality. The Sage the Quick Touch takes it further with 1000W inverter power and a flatbed design.

1591|
1592| 1593|
1594|

Flatbed vs Turntable: Which Design Wins?

1595|

Most microwaves use a rotating glass turntable to move food through the microwave field for even cooking. Flatbed models use a rotating antenna beneath the ceramic floor to distribute microwaves evenly instead.

1596|
    1597|
  • Flatbed advantages: More usable interior space (no turntable to work around), easier to clean (flat surface, no mechanical parts), can fit rectangular or oddly shaped dishes. The Sage the Quick Touch is our recommended flatbed model.
    • 1598|
  • Turntable advantages: Significantly cheaper, more models to choose from, the rotation does a reliable job of even cooking. All four of our other recommended models use turntables.
    • 1599|
1600|

For most people, the difference is marginal. If you regularly cook large rectangular dishes (lasagne, casseroles), a flatbed is a genuine upgrade. For everyday reheating of plates of food, a turntable works perfectly well.

Energy Efficiency: Microwaves Are the Cheapest Appliance to Run

1605|

If you're watching your energy bills, microwaves are your friend. An 800W microwave used for 5 minutes costs approximately 2p at the current UK energy price cap (~25p per kWh). Compare that to an electric oven which costs around 30-40p for the same task, and the savings add up quickly — especially if you use your microwave regularly for cooking vegetables, reheating, and defrosting rather than leaving the hob or oven on.

1606|

A microwave can't do everything an oven can (no crispy roast potatoes, sadly), but for speed and energy efficiency, nothing beats it. If you're replacing an old microwave, the efficiency difference between new models is negligible — they all convert electricity to microwave energy with similar efficiency. The bigger energy saving comes from using the microwave instead of your oven whenever practical.
