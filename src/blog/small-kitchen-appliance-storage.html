Small Kitchen Storage: The Best Space-Saving Appliances for UK Kitchens

The average UK kitchen is just 13m² — among the smallest in Europe. We rank which appliances earn their counter space and which are gathering dust after two uses.

📅 13 May 2026 ⏱ 9 min read ✍️ Home & Kitchen UK

📐 The Short Answer

The appliances that genuinely earn their permanent counter spot in a small UK kitchen are: a good kettle (used 4–8 times daily), an air fryer (replaces the oven for most meals), a toaster (if you eat toast daily), and a multi-cooker like an Instant Pot (replaces 3+ separate appliances). Most everything else — bread makers, rice cookers, juicers — belongs in a cupboard or shouldn't be bought at all.

The UK Kitchen: Small by European Standards

Let's put the numbers on the table. According to the Royal Institute of British Architects (RIBA), the average UK kitchen measures just 13 square metres. That's smaller than the European average of 16m² and positively cramped compared to countries like Germany (18m²) or the Netherlands (20m²). In new-build flats, kitchens can be as small as 6–9m² — barely enough room for two people to stand, let alone accommodate a collection of single-purpose gadgets.

And yet British kitchens are expected to do everything: breakfast, packed lunches, weeknight dinners, Sunday roasts, baking, entertaining. Every appliance that sits on the worktop is competing for perhaps 1.5–3 square metres of usable counter space. The question isn't "is this appliance useful?" — it's "is this appliance more useful than the empty space it occupies?"

📏 UK Kitchen Facts

Average UK kitchen: 13m² (140 sq ft)

New-build flat kitchen: 6–9m² (65–97 sq ft)

Average usable counter space: ~2–3 linear metres, much of it already occupied by a draining board, fruit bowl, and the things you can't put away.

Source: RIBA, English Housing Survey, LABC Warranty data.

The Essential Four: Why These Earn Their Space

★ Essential

🫖 Kettle

In the UK, a kettle is non-negotiable. The average Brit boils a kettle 4–6 times per day — for tea, coffee, hot water bottles, cooking pasta, and that second cuppa you promised yourself. It uses the counter for maybe 20×22cm and there is simply no alternative that works as quickly. The only question is whether to store it on the counter or on the draining board — and the answer is counter, always. A kettle on the draining board gets splashed and knocked.

Space-saving tip: A compact 1.5L kettle (like the Bosch Styline or Russell Hobbs Compact) takes up less room than a 1.7L jug kettle without meaningfully reducing capacity for 1–2 people.

★ Essential

🍟 Air Fryer

Yes, an air fryer is a counter-hog — a dual-basket model like the Ninja Foodi MAX takes up roughly 31×41cm. But here's the calculation: if you use it to replace your electric oven for 80% of meals, you're saving roughly £60 per year in electricity (see our air fryer running costs guide) and freeing up oven space for the few meals that genuinely need it. For a family of four, an air fryer is used at least once daily — that's a better counter-to-usage ratio than almost any other appliance.

Space-saving tip: A single-basket 3.8–4.2L air fryer (Ninja AF100UK, Tefal Easy Fry) has a footprint of ~26×32cm — about the size of a dinner plate — and feeds 1–2 people perfectly.

★ Essential

🍞 Toaster

You can make toast under the grill, but it's slower, less even, and uses your entire oven. A 2-slice toaster costs £20–40, takes 18×25cm of counter space, and is used every morning in toast-eating households. If you don't eat toast, don't buy a toaster — but if you do, it earns its space. A 4-slice toaster is only worth it for families of 3+ who regularly eat toast together; otherwise the extra 10cm of width isn't justified.

★ Worth It

🍲 Multi-Cooker (Instant Pot / Ninja Speedi)

This is the dark horse of space-efficient appliances. A single Instant Pot Duo (31×28cm) replaces: a slow cooker, a rice cooker, a pressure cooker, a steamer, a yoghurt maker, and a sauté pan. If you currently own even two of those, consolidating into a multi-cooker frees up counter space, not consumes it. And pressure cooking is genuinely fast — a whole chicken in 25 minutes, dried beans in 30 minutes without soaking. For batch-cooking households, it's transformative.

Space-saving tip: The Instant Pot Duo Mini (3L, 26×22cm) feeds 1–3 people and fits into even the tiniest kitchen. The Ninja Speedi (10-in-1) adds an air fryer function, potentially replacing two appliances in one footprint.

The 'It Depends' Category

◉ Depends

☕ Coffee Machine

A bean-to-cup coffee machine is a significant investment in space (and money — £300–800). But if you're currently spending £3.40 on a latte from Costa or Caffè Nero five days a week, that's £884 per year. A £400 Sage Barista Express pays for itself in under 6 months on those numbers. If you drink instant coffee at home and only have "proper" coffee at weekends, a French press (£15) or AeroPress (£30) produces excellent results with zero counter footprint.

◉ Depends

📡 Microwave

A microwave is the largest single appliance most people keep on the counter (35×45cm). If you genuinely use it multiple times daily for reheating, defrosting, and jacket potatoes, it earns its spot. But if you only use it to reheat the occasional cup of tea and melt butter, consider downgrading to a compact 17L model (30×30cm) or mounting it on a dedicated shelf or under-cabinet bracket. A microwave that sits unused for days at a time is the single biggest waste of counter space in most UK kitchens.

The 'Skip It' Rogues' Gallery

These are the appliances that are bought with good intentions, used enthusiastically for two weeks, then pushed to the back of the worktop to gather a fine layer of cooking grease until being relegated to a cupboard — or the charity shop.

✗ Skip It

🍞 Bread Maker

The bread maker is the poster child for "used twice, now a £60 paperweight." Yes, fresh bread smells wonderful. But a bread maker takes up 30×35cm of permanent counter space for something you'll use once a week at most. Making bread by hand (or in a stand mixer if you already own one) and baking it in the oven produces better results with zero extra appliance footprint. If you genuinely want fresh bread daily, buy a bread maker — but be honest with yourself first.

✗ Skip It

🍚 Standalone Rice Cooker

In many East Asian kitchens, a rice cooker is essential and used daily. In most UK kitchens, rice is cooked 1–3 times a week and a saucepan on the hob does the job perfectly well. A rice cooker's one advantage — set-and-forget consistency — is real, but it's not worth 22×22cm of permanent counter space unless you eat rice almost every day. If you must have one, a multi-cooker (Instant Pot) does rice cooking plus everything else.

✗ Skip It

🧃 Juicer

Juicers are the ultimate "January purchase, February cupboard exile." The cleaning time (disassembling, scrubbing the mesh filter, washing the pulp collector) consistently exceeds the drinking time. A centrifugal juicer takes up 20×25cm and most are used for approximately three weeks before the novelty wears off. If you want juice, buy it — or use a blender and strain through a muslin cloth.

Smart Storage Solutions for the Space You Have

📦 Vertical Stacking Shelves

A two-tier wire shelf (available from IKEA, Amazon, or Lakeland for £15–25) lets you stack a smaller appliance (kettle, toaster) above a larger one (air fryer, microwave). The key: put the heavier, less frequently moved appliance on the bottom and the daily-use one on top. Make sure the shelf is rated for the weight — a 2.5kg kettle is fine; a 5kg stand mixer needs something sturdier.

🚪 Under-Cabinet Appliance Garage

An appliance garage is a cupboard that sits on the counter under your wall cabinets, with a roller shutter or lift-up door. It hides the toaster, kettle, and coffee machine when not in use, keeping the worktop visually clear. Hafele and DIY Kitchens sell units from £80–200. The trade-off: you lose 35–40cm of counter depth, so only install one if you have at least 60cm of depth available.

🔪 Magnetic Knife Strip

A magnetic knife strip on the wall frees an entire drawer that was previously full of a knife block. That drawer can now hold smaller appliances (hand blender, spice grinder, stick blender) that previously cluttered the counter. Cost: £10–25 from IKEA, Amazon, or any kitchen shop. Installation: two screws into the wall — no drilling if you use heavy-duty adhesive strips.

📐 Fold-Down Worktop Extension

A hinged shelf that folds flat against the wall when not in use, then lifts up to create an extra 40–60cm of worktop for food prep or temporary appliance use. Ideal for kitchens where you prep food in one area but need occasional extra space. Wall-mounted brackets and a solid wood or laminate shelf cost £30–60.

🪝 Under-Shelf Hooks and Rails

Hooks mounted under wall cabinets can hold mugs, utensils, and even small baskets for tea bags and coffee pods — freeing drawer and counter space. A stainless steel rail with S-hooks costs £10–20 and takes 10 minutes to install.

The Golden Rule: One In, One Out

If you bring a new appliance into your kitchen, ask yourself: what is this replacing? Not "what else can I fit on the counter?" — what is being donated, sold, or moved to a cupboard to make space? If the answer is "nothing," you probably don't need the new appliance badly enough.

Kitchen counters are not storage. They're workspace. Every square centimetre occupied by a rarely-used gadget is a square centimetre you can't use for chopping vegetables, rolling pastry, or plating up dinner. The best small-kitchen appliance is the one you use today — not the one you might use at Christmas.
