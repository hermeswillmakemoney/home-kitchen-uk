How to Choose the Right Kitchen Knives

Whether you’re building your first knife collection or upgrading tired blades, understanding the key factors will help you make a confident choice. Here’s what to consider before buying.

The Chef’s Knife: Your Kitchen Workhorse

An 8-inch (20cm) chef’s knife is the single most important knife in any kitchen. It handles the vast majority of cutting tasks: chopping vegetables, slicing meat, mincing herbs, and even crushing garlic with the flat of the blade. If you only buy one knife, make it a quality chef’s knife. Our top pick, the Wusthof Classic Ikon, delivers the best overall experience, while the Victorinox Fibrox Pro proves you don’t need to spend big for professional-grade performance.

Blade Steel and Hardness (HRC)

The Rockwell hardness scale (HRC) measures how hard a blade’s steel is. Higher numbers mean better edge retention but can make the blade more brittle and harder to sharpen. The knives in our roundup range from 56 to 58 HRC:

  • 56 HRC (Victorinox): Softer steel, easier to sharpen at home, but needs more frequent honing.
  • 57 HRC (ZWILLING): A well-balanced middle ground — good edge retention with reasonable sharpenability.
  • 58 HRC (Wusthof): Premium hardness for excellent edge retention, best suited to those comfortable with regular honing.

Japanese stainless steel (as used in the Joseph Joseph set) tends to be harder and thinner, excelling at precision cuts but requiring more careful handling.

Full Tang vs Partial Tang

A full tang means the blade steel extends the full length of the handle, providing superior balance and durability. The Wusthof Classic Ikon and ZWILLING Professional S are both precision-forged from a single piece of steel — the gold standard for construction quality. Stamped blades (cut from a sheet of steel) are lighter and cheaper but can feel less substantial. For a knife you’ll use daily, full tang is worth the investment.

Handle Materials

The handle affects comfort, hygiene, and longevity:

  • POM (Polyoxymethylene): Used on the Wusthof Classic Ikon. Dense, durable, and resistant to moisture and chemicals. The premium choice.
  • Polypropylene: Used on the ZWILLING Professional S. Lightweight, hygienic, and dishwasher-safe, though less luxurious to hold.
  • Fibrox (thermoplastic): Used on the Victorinox. Slip-resistant texture makes it ideal for professional kitchens, though it lacks aesthetic appeal.
  • PP with soft-grip: Used on the Joseph Joseph set. Comfortable for everyday use at an accessible price point.

Knife Sets vs Individual Knives

A knife block set like the Joseph Joseph Duo 5pc is ideal if you’re equipping a kitchen from scratch or want a coordinated collection with convenient storage. However, individual knives from premium brands often outperform set knives in terms of blade quality and comfort. Many serious cooks prefer to build their collection piece by piece, starting with a great chef’s knife and adding a paring knife, bread knife, and utility knife over time.

Care and Maintenance

A quality knife is only as good as how you care for it:

  • Hand-wash only. Dishwasher detergents and heat will damage blade edges and handles over time.
  • Hone regularly. Use a honing steel every few uses to realign the edge. This is different from sharpening.
  • Sharpen periodically. Depending on use, professional sharpening every 6-12 months will keep blades in top condition.
  • Store properly. Use a knife block, magnetic strip, or blade guards. Never toss knives loose in a drawer.
  • Use the right surface. Always cut on wood or composite boards. Glass, marble, and ceramic will dull blades rapidly.

Frequently Asked Questions

A chef’s knife is the single most versatile and important knife in any kitchen. An 8-inch (20cm) chef’s knife can handle the vast majority of cutting tasks, from chopping vegetables to slicing meat and mincing herbs. Investing in a quality chef’s knife — like the Wusthof Classic Ikon or Victorinox Fibrox Pro — should be your first priority. Everything else is a complement to this essential tool.

Quality knives from reputable brands like Wusthof and ZWILLING use superior steel that holds an edge longer, feature better balance and ergonomics, and can last decades with proper care. While budget options like the Victorinox Fibrox Pro deliver excellent performance at lower prices, premium knives offer refinements in feel, edge retention, and longevity that many serious cooks find worthwhile. Think of it as a buy-once investment rather than a recurring expense.

The best storage options are knife blocks, magnetic strips, or in-drawer knife trays. A knife block set like the Joseph Joseph Duo 5pc keeps everything organised and protected on your worktop. Never store knives loose in a drawer where blades can knock against each other — this dulls edges and creates a safety hazard. If you don’t have a block, individual blade guards are an affordable alternative.

For regular home use, honing with a steel every few uses will keep the edge aligned and cutting effectively. A full sharpening (using a whetstone or professional service) is typically needed every 6-12 months depending on usage. Higher-hardness steels like those in Wusthof knives (58 HRC) hold their edge longer than softer steels but are more challenging to sharpen at home. The Victorinox, at 56 HRC, is the easiest to sharpen yourself.

We strongly recommend against putting quality kitchen knives in the dishwasher. The harsh detergents, high heat, and knocking against other items will dull the blade, damage handles, and can cause corrosion over time. Hand-wash knives with warm soapy water and dry immediately for the best longevity. Even knives marketed as dishwasher-safe — like the Victorinox Fibrox Pro — will last significantly longer with hand-washing.

German knives (like Wusthof and ZWILLING) are typically heavier, with thicker blades and a more pronounced curve — ideal for rock-chopping. They use softer steel (56-58 HRC) that’s forgiving to sharpen. Japanese knives tend to be lighter and thinner, with harder steel (60+ HRC) that holds a razor-sharp edge but is more brittle. The Joseph Joseph Duo set uses Japanese stainless steel, offering a lighter, precision-oriented cutting experience. Neither is better — it depends on your cutting style and preference.
